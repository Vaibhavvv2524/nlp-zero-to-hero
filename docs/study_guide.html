Part 0 — Orientation

1. The assignment in plain English

You are given Hindi text with three problems:

  1. No punctuation and no sentence boundaries. The words arrive in a flat stream. You must decide where commas, full stops (the Devanagari danda ।), question marks, and exclamation marks belong.
  2. The sentences are shuffled. Even after you recover the boundaries, the sentences are not in their original order. You must recover a sensible order.
  3. The text must be translated into English, and the translation must preserve the grouping.

The required deliverables are, for each input file: the Hindi sentences in recovered order, the same sentences grouped into blocks of five, the English translation of each sentence, and the English groups.

A hard constraint is attached: no LLM may be used, and the whole pipeline must fit in a 1.5 GB GPU memory budget. That is why the project uses two small, task-specific model families rather than an instruction-following chat model.

The final architecture is:

Stage Question answered Model Output
1. Punctuation Does this word end a sentence, take a comma, or take no mark? bert-base-multilingual-cased + LoRA token classifier one label per word
2. Ordering Given sentences A and B, does A come before B? bert-base-multilingual-cased + LoRA pair classifier probability per pair, decoded into a full order
3. Translation What is the English for this Hindi sentence? ai4bharat/indictrans2-indic-en-dist-200M English sentence

Key idea. The project is not one model. It is three models plus a data pipeline, glued together by a small amount of careful engineering. Most of the difficulty is in the data representation, the label alignment, and the decoding logic, not in the model code.

2. The seven inputs and the four outputs

The raw inputs live in data/raw/sample-1.txt through sample-7.txt. Each file is space-separated tokens. The assignment's tokenizer has replaced some words with the literal token NULL; those must be removed before modelling. The files contain no punctuation and no sentence order information.

After python -m src.pipeline, each input produces four files:

Output file What it contains
outputs/sample-N.sentences_hi.txt one punctuated Hindi sentence per line, in recovered order
outputs/sample-N.groups_hi.txt one line per group of five Hindi sentences
outputs/sample-N.sentences_en.txt one English translation per Hindi sentence, same order
outputs/sample-N.groups_en.txt English translations joined into the same groups of five

The produced counts are:

The last group in a file is allowed to contain fewer than five sentences. Grouping is purely positional: sentences 1-5 become group 1, sentences 6-10 become group 2, and so on.

Why sample-6 and sample-7 have so many sentences. Those files contain dialogue-like text with many short utterances. The punctuation model learned sentence endings from Wikipedia, where complete sentences are usually at least five words long. Short utterances therefore look like sentence endings to the model, and the file is over-segmented. This is a real limitation, not a bug in the grouping code.

This is what makes the 1.5 GB budget plausible on a T4: the budget applies to the largest single stage, not to the sum of all three.

Part 0.5 — The approach we chose (read this first)

APPROACH CHOSEN — the thesis of the whole project.
We deliberately decomposed the assignment into three specialized stages instead of asking one model to do everything: a word-level punctuation labeller, a pairwise sentence-order scorer with an explicit decoder, and a dedicated Indic-to-English translator. The first two stages share one small pretrained backbone (mBERT) and are adapted with LoRA; the third is IndicTrans2 200M. There is no LLM, no fallback model, and no stage is allowed to rewrite the input words. The pipeline is engineered around the 1.5 GB budget by loading exactly one model at a time.

This chapter is intentionally placed before the theory. If you understand why this architecture was chosen, every later chapter has a place to hang. The rest of the guide explains the machinery; this chapter explains the design.

A.1 Why decompose the problem at all?

A single end-to-end model could, in principle, read the flat token stream and emit punctuated, ordered English. We rejected that for four reasons:

Reason Explanation
Constraint fit A single generative model large enough to do all three tasks well would not fit 1.5 GB and would look like an LLM, which the assignment forbids.
Evaluation Decomposition gives one metric per stage: boundary F1, pair F1/Kendall's tau, BLEU/chrF. A single model gives one opaque score, and a failure is hard to localize.
Data availability Wikipedia gives us punctuation labels and natural sentence order for free. It does not give us aligned triplets of (unpunctuated shuffled Hindi, punctuated ordered Hindi, English). Decomposition lets us train each stage on the supervision that actually exists.
Failure isolation If ordering is weak, punctuation and translation can still be correct and measurable. If translation is unavailable, the first two stages still produce a valid partial result.

The decomposition is not an accident of convenience. It is the design that makes the constraints, the data, and the evaluation line up.

A.2 The eight principles behind the chosen approach

**1. Preserve the words; predict structure.** Punctuation is a labelling problem, not a rewriting problem. Stage 1 attaches one of five labels to each existing word. This guarantees that the output words are the input words (minus `NULL`) and makes word-level evaluation exact. **2. Learn from natural text, not synthetic text.** The training corpus is Hindi Wikipedia. The punctuation and sentence order are already there; we only hide them to create supervision. This avoids hand-written rules and avoids LLM-generated data. **3. Split by article; shuffle inside windows.** The same article never appears in both training and test. Inside a punctuation window, sentences are shuffled to mimic the assignment input and to stop the model from leaning on cross-sentence continuity. **4. Reuse one backbone for two tasks.** mBERT is used once as a token classifier and once as a pair classifier. This keeps the dependency surface small, the memory profile predictable, and the story simple: one multilingual encoder, two heads. **5. Adapt cheaply with LoRA.** Full fine-tuning of 178M parameters is unnecessary and risky under the budget. LoRA trains about 0.33% of the parameters, keeps optimizer state tiny, and produces adapters small enough to save and share. **6. Make local decisions, then decode globally.** The ordering model answers only “does A come before B?”. A separate decoder (Borda by default) turns all pairwise probabilities into a global permutation. This separates learning from combinatorial optimization and lets us compare decoders without retraining. **7. Use a dedicated translator, sentence by sentence.** IndicTrans2 is specialized for Indic languages and small enough for the budget. Translating sentence by sentence preserves exact alignment with the Hindi sentences and the groups of five. **8. Stage memory, fail loudly, report honestly.** Load one model, use it, free it. If a model or adapter is missing, raise a clear error; never silently switch to a fallback. Report what was actually run and distinguish it from what is merely documented.

A.3 The approach at a glance

Stage Input Chosen model Chosen decoder Why this choice
1. Punctuation flat Hindi word list mBERT + LoRA token classifier (5 labels) argmax over triangular-weighted window probabilities one decision per word; preserves words; cheap; strong sentence-ending cues
2. Ordering list of plain Hindi sentences mBERT + LoRA pair classifier (2 labels) Borda over the full pairwise probability matrix pairwise model generalizes to any length; Borda averages out local errors
3. Translation ordered Hindi sentences IndicTrans2 200M encoder-decoder beam search (4 beams) Indic-specialized, fits budget, sentence alignment is exact
Orchestration seven raw files none load/free one stage at a time keeps the largest resident model under 1.5 GB

The data flow is:

raw tokens -> [stage 1: label each word] -> sentences
           -> [stage 2: score all pairs + Borda] -> ordered sentences
           -> [stage 3: translate each sentence] -> aligned English
           -> group every five sentences

A.4 What we explicitly rejected

Rejected approach Why it was rejected

A.6 Why this approach is defensible in a viva

  • Every design choice has a stated alternative. The table above is the short version; Chapter 28 expands it.
  • Every stage has its own metric. You can defend punctuation without defending ordering, and ordering without defending translation.
  • The weaknesses are measured, not hidden. Comma F1, question/exclamation F1, and ordering tau are reported honestly.
  • The memory claim is architectural, not wishful. The code physically releases each model before loading the next.
  • The data story is clean. Natural Wikipedia supervision, article-level splits, and no synthetic labels.
If you remember one thing: the project succeeds because it chooses the right decomposition for the constraints. mBERT labels words, a pairwise model ranks sentences, a decoder turns rankings into an order, and IndicTrans2 translates. Each stage is small, measurable, and replaceable.

Part I — NLP foundations from zero

5. What is natural language processing?

Natural language processing is the study of how to make computers do useful things with human language. “Useful things” includes classification (is this review positive?), extraction (who did what to whom?), generation (translate this sentence), retrieval (find the passage that answers this question), and structuring (turn a flat stream of words into sentences and paragraphs).

Language is hard for computers for reasons that are obvious to humans and invisible to code:

  • Ambiguity. “I saw the man with the telescope” has two meanings. बैंक can mean a river bank or a financial bank.
  • Context dependence. The meaning of a word depends on the sentence, the paragraph, and the world.
  • Compositionality. The meaning of a sentence is built from its parts, but not by simple addition.
  • Variation. Dialects, spelling variants, code-mixing, and noise.
  • Long-range structure. A pronoun on page two may refer to a name on page one.

Classical NLP decomposed the problem into levels:

Level Studies Example
Phonology Sounds pronunciation, speech recognition
Morphology Word structure Hindi verb conjugation, plurals
Syntax Sentence structure subject-verb-object order
Semantics Meaning entailment, word sense
Pragmatics Meaning in context sarcasm, implicature
Discourse Structure beyond a sentence pronoun resolution, paragraph order

This project touches morphology (Hindi is agglutinative and has rich inflection), syntax (sentence boundaries depend on clause structure), semantics (ordering depends on meaning and discourse), and generation (translation). It does not attempt speech or dialogue.

5.1 Rule-based, statistical, neural, and LLM approaches

The history of NLP is a sequence of increasingly flexible representations:

  1. Rule-based. Hand-written grammars and dictionaries. Predictable, explainable, brittle. The legacy baseline/ folder is in this tradition: dynamic-programming segmentation using verb endings, sentence starters, NULL hints, and a length prior.
  2. Statistical. Counts and probabilities learned from data: n-gram language models, hidden Markov models, TF-IDF plus logistic regression. The lessons/train.py exercise is a tiny example of this family.
  3. Neural. Dense vectors and gradient-based learning: recurrent networks, convolutional networks, and then transformers. The project's two mBERT stages are here.
  4. Large language models. Very large transformers trained on broad text, often used through prompting or instruction tuning. The assignment explicitly forbids this family, so the project does not use one.

Why forbid LLMs? The assignment is designed to test whether you can build a pipeline from task-specific components: data preparation, token classification, pairwise ranking, and translation. Asking a chat model to “fix this text” would hide all of that. It would also make the memory and reproducibility claims meaningless.

5.2 Where this project sits

Assignment requirement Technique used Why
Restore punctuation and boundaries token classification with mBERT + LoRA one decision per word; preserves the original words; small and fast
Recover sentence order pairwise classification plus a decoder order is a global structure, but a pairwise model generalizes to any number of sentences
Translate IndicTrans2 encoder-decoder dedicated Hindi-to-English model, much smaller than an LLM, strong on Indic languages
Fit 1.5 GB fp16 on CUDA, LoRA, one stage at a time keeps the largest resident model under the cap

Check yourself. Why is punctuation restoration formulated as token classification rather than as translation? Because we do not want to rewrite the words; we want to attach one of five labels to each existing word. Translation-style generation would risk changing the text and would be far more expensive.

6. From text to tokens

6.1 Text is Unicode, not bytes

A Python str is a sequence of Unicode code points. Each code point has a number. Hindi's Devanagari script occupies the range U+0900 to U+097F. Important characters in this project:

Character Code point Name Role
। U+0964 Devanagari danda sentence end
॥ U+0965 Devanagari double danda verse/section end; treated as sentence end here
? U+003F question mark question
! U+0021 exclamation mark exclamation
, U+002C comma comma

Devanagari is not a simple alphabet. Consonants have an inherent vowel, and vowel signs (matras) are separate combining characters that attach to the consonant. For example, अयोध्या is six code points:

अ  U+0905  DEVANAGARI LETTER A
य  U+092F  DEVANAGARI LETTER YA
ो  U+094B  DEVANAGARI VOWEL SIGN O      (combining)
ध  U+0927  DEVANAGARI LETTER DHA
्  U+094D  DEVANAGARI SIGN VIRAMA       (combining)
य  U+092F  DEVANAGARI LETTER YA
ा  U+093E  DEVANAGARI VOWEL SIGN AA      (combining)

The visual word is one grapheme cluster, but the string has multiple code points. This matters for any regex or tokenizer that assumes “one code point = one letter”.

Common mistake. Using re.findall(r'\w+', text) on Hindi. In Python, \w matches letters and digits but not all combining marks, so matras are silently dropped:

>>> import re
>>> re.findall(r'\w+', 'अयोध्या')
['अय', 'ध', 'य']
>>> re.findall(r'\w+', 'महालक्ष्मी')
['मह', 'लक', 'ष', 'म']

This is exactly why src/data_prep.py uses str.split() and src/utils.py implements strip_punctuation() without a word regex. Splitting on whitespace preserves every code point inside a token.

6.2 Word tokenization

Word tokenization splits a string into words. In English, whitespace plus punctuation rules are enough for many purposes; NLTK's word_tokenize additionally separates clitics and punctuation. In Hindi, whitespace is a strong signal because Hindi orthography separates words with spaces, but punctuation can be glued to the final word (हुआ।), and compounds may use hyphens (राज्य-प्रबन्धन).

The project's tokenizer is deliberately minimal:

raw_tokens = text.split()
words = [token for token in raw_tokens if token != 'NULL']

Then, during data preparation, strip_punctuation() separates a trailing mark from the word and records it as a label. This is a reversible tokenization: the punctuation is stored as a label rather than thrown away, and restore_punctuation() can put it back.

6.3 Sentence tokenization

Sentence splitting is harder. In English, a period can end a sentence or an abbreviation. In Hindi, the danda is a strong signal, but a danda can also appear in abbreviations or verse numbering. The project uses a regex lookbehind:

ARTICLE_PATTERN = re.compile(r'(?<=[।॥?!])\s+')

This splits at whitespace after a sentence-final mark. It is simple and works well for Wikipedia. It does not handle every edge case, which is acceptable because this is training-data extraction, not the final inference path. At inference time, sentence boundaries come from the trained classifier, not from this regex.

6.4 Subword tokenization

Modern transformer models rarely use whole words. A vocabulary of all Hindi words would be enormous and would still miss new words. Instead, models use subword tokenization: frequent words stay whole, rare words are split into smaller pieces.

The three main algorithms:

  • BPE (Byte Pair Encoding). Start with characters (or bytes). Repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol. This builds a vocabulary of frequent pieces.
  • WordPiece. Similar in spirit, but merges are chosen by a likelihood criterion rather than raw frequency. Continuation pieces are marked with ##. This is what BERT and mBERT use.
  • Unigram / SentencePiece. Start with many candidate pieces and prune the vocabulary probabilistically. SentencePiece treats the input as a raw character stream, which is convenient for languages without spaces. IndicTrans2 uses a SentencePiece-style vocabulary.

A useful mental model: subword tokenization is a compression scheme learned from data. Common strings become single tokens; rare strings become several tokens. The model never sees an “unknown word” in the classical sense; it sees pieces.

A real mBERT example

The sentence भारत एक विशाल देश है। becomes:

[CLS] भारत एक वि ##शा ##ल देश है । [SEP]

The word विशाल is split into वि, ##शा, ##ल; the ## means “this piece continues the previous word”. Other examples:

Word mBERT pieces
अयोध्या अ, ##य, ##ोध, ##्या
राज्य-प्रबन्धन राज्य, -, प, ##्र, ##बन्ध, ##न
महालक्ष्मी म, ##हा, ##ल, ##क्ष, ##्मी

Why this matters for labelling. The punctuation label belongs to a word, but the model predicts a label for each subword. The project's dataset puts the word's label on the first subword and sets every continuation subword to -100, which the loss ignores. That way each word is supervised exactly once. See src/punctuation/dataset.py:24-43.

Special tokens, padding, truncation, attention masks

  • [CLS] is a special classification token at the start. BERT's final hidden state for [CLS] is used as a sentence-level summary in sequence classification.
  • [SEP] separates sequences. For a pair, the format is [CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP].
  • [PAD] fills shorter sequences in a batch so they have equal length.
  • Attention mask is 1 for real tokens and 0 for padding, so the model ignores padding.
  • Truncation cuts a sequence longer than the model's maximum length (128 for punctuation windows, 192 for sentence pairs).
  • word_ids() maps each subword back to its original word index, with None for special tokens. This is the bridge between word-level labels and subword-level predictions.
encoding = tokenizer(words, is_split_into_words=True, truncation=True,
                     max_length=128)
word_ids = encoding.word_ids()   # e.g. [None, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, None]

Check yourself. Why can't we simply label every subword with the word's punctuation label? Because then a five-subword word would contribute five identical training signals, and at inference time the same word would produce five possibly conflicting predictions. First-subword labelling gives one prediction per word and keeps the loss balanced.

7. Embeddings and vector representations

A model cannot do arithmetic on the string भारत. It needs numbers. An embedding is a dense vector that represents a token, word, sentence, or document.

APPROACH CHOSEN. We do not train embeddings from scratch. We reuse mBERT's contextual embeddings, and we use TF-IDF only where a cheap lexical representation is enough (the ordering ablation and the clustering decoder). This keeps the learned representation strong and the extra machinery minimal.

7.1 One-hot and bag-of-words

The simplest representation is a vector with one dimension per vocabulary item. For a 100,000-word vocabulary, भारत might be a vector of 99,999 zeros and one one. This is interpretable but enormous, sparse, and semantically empty: भारत and भारतीय are as far apart as भारत and banana.

A bag of words counts word occurrences in a document and ignores order. It is the basis of TF-IDF.

7.2 TF-IDF

TF-IDF weights a term by how often it appears in a document, discounted by how common it is across all documents:

tf-idf(t, d) = tf(t, d) * log(N / df(t))
  • tf(t,d) is the frequency of term t in document d.
  • df(t) is the number of documents containing t.
  • N is the number of documents.

A term that appears everywhere (like “the”) gets a low weight; a term that is rare but appears in this document gets a high weight. The project uses TF-IDF in two places:

  • Ordering ablation: a TF-IDF similarity matrix plus a greedy order, with no mBERT. This is the “can simple lexical similarity do the job?” baseline.
  • Clustering decoder: character n-gram TF-IDF (analyzer='char_wb', 2- to 4-grams) plus KMeans to group topically similar sentences before ordering.

Character n-grams are useful for Hindi because they capture morphology and tolerate spelling variation better than whole-word features. char_wb means n-grams are built within word boundaries, with padding at the edges.

7.3 Static word embeddings

Word2vec, GloVe, and fastText learn a vector per word from co-occurrence statistics:

  • Word2vec (skip-gram). Predict context words from a target word. The learned input vectors become the embeddings. The objective for a word w_t and context window c is to maximize the sum of log p(w_{t+j} | w_t) over nearby context positions.
  • GloVe. Factorizes a global co-occurrence matrix so that vector differences encode meaning relations.
  • fastText. Represents a word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors, so it can handle unseen words and morphology.

These embeddings are static: the vector for बैंक is the same in every sentence. That is a fundamental limitation.

7.4 Contextual embeddings

Transformers produce contextual representations. The vector for a token is computed from the token plus its entire context, layer by layer. In BERT, the final hidden state for each token is a 768-dimensional vector that depends on the sentence. The same word in two different sentences can have different vectors.

For a sentence-level task, BERT's [CLS] vector is a learned summary of the whole sequence. For a token-level task, each token's final hidden state is passed through a classification head.

Choice: mBERT contextual embeddings for both learned stages, not static word vectors. Reason: punctuation depends on syntax and local context; ordering depends on the relationship between two sentences. Static vectors cannot represent either. Cost: a 178M-parameter backbone, which we fit with LoRA and fp16.

7.5 Cosine similarity

To compare two vectors, the standard measure is cosine similarity:

cos(u, v) = (u . v) / (||u|| * ||v||)

It measures the angle between vectors and ignores magnitude. It is used in the clustering decoder to choose a medoid (the member closest to the cluster centroid) and in the TF-IDF ablation.

Check yourself. Why does the project use contextual mBERT embeddings for ordering but TF-IDF for clustering? TF-IDF is cheap and good enough to group topically similar sentences; the expensive mBERT pair model is reserved for the finer before/after decisions. The evaluation compares this hybrid against a TF-IDF-only ablation.

8. Transformers and BERT

APPROACH CHOSEN. Both learned stages use the same encoder-only backbone, mBERT, with a small task-specific head. Stage 1 uses token classification; stage 2 uses sequence classification. The translation stage is the only encoder-decoder. We deliberately avoid a single multi-task model because separate heads are easier to train, evaluate, and reason about.

8.1 The basic neural network

A neural network is a stack of layers that transform vectors. A linear layer computes y = Wx + b. A nonlinearity such as ReLU or GELU lets the network learn curved decision boundaries. A loss function measures how wrong the output is; backpropagation computes gradients; an optimizer updates the weights.

For classification, the model produces logits z, one per class. Softmax turns them into probabilities:

p_i = exp(z_i) / sum_j exp(z_j)

The cross-entropy loss for the correct class y is -log(p_y). If the model is confident and correct, the loss is near zero. If it is confident and wrong, the loss is large. This is the loss used in both mBERT stages (with class weights in punctuation).

8.2 Attention

The central operation of a transformer is scaled dot-product attention:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q K^T / sqrt(d_k)) V
  • Q (query), K (key), and V (value) are linear projections of the input.
  • Q K^T computes a score for every pair of positions: how much should position i attend to position j?
  • Dividing by sqrt(d_k) keeps the scores from growing too large as the dimension grows.
  • Softmax turns the scores into attention weights.
  • Multiplying by V produces a weighted sum of value vectors.

Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel with different projections, then concatenates and projects the results. Different heads can learn different relationships: syntax, coreference, local order, and so on.

A transformer block adds:

  • Residual connections: x + f(x), which make deep networks trainable.
  • Layer normalization: stabilizes activations.
  • Feed-forward network: a two-layer MLP applied to each position independently, usually with a hidden size 4 times the model dimension.
  • Dropout: randomly zeroes activations during training to reduce overfitting.

8.3 Encoder, decoder, and sequence-to-sequence

  • An encoder reads the whole input and produces contextual representations. BERT is an encoder.
  • A decoder generates output one token at a time, attending to what it has already generated and, in a sequence-to-sequence model, to the encoder's output.
  • A sequence-to-sequence model combines both. IndicTrans2 is an encoder-decoder: the encoder reads Hindi, the decoder generates English.

BERT uses bidirectional attention: every token can attend to every other token. This is great for understanding. It is not directly suited to generation, because generation must not see the future. GPT-style models use causal attention: each token can attend only to earlier tokens.

8.4 BERT and mBERT

BERT is pretrained with two objectives:

  1. Masked language modelling (MLM). Randomly mask some tokens and predict them from context. This forces the model to learn syntax and semantics.
  2. Next sentence prediction (NSP). Predict whether sentence B follows sentence A. NSP is less important in modern practice, but it is part of the original BERT.

bert-base-multilingual-cased is the multilingual version, trained on Wikipedia in over 100 languages. Its configuration:

8.5 Task heads

A pretrained encoder is a backbone. To use it, we attach a head:

  • Token classification: a linear layer maps each token's 768-dimensional hidden state to 5 logits (one per punctuation label).
  • Sequence classification: the [CLS] vector is mapped to 2 logits (wrong order / correct order).
  • Sequence-to-sequence: the encoder-decoder generates token by token; no simple classification head.

The head is randomly initialized and trained from scratch. This is why the notebook prints “Some weights of BertForTokenClassification were not initialized from the model checkpoint” when loading the base model. That warning is expected: the backbone is pretrained, the head is new.

8.6 Why mBERT for this project

  • It is multilingual and has seen Hindi during pretraining.
  • It is small enough (about 178M parameters) to fit the budget with LoRA and fp16.
  • It is widely available, well documented, and works with the pinned transformers version.
  • It supports both token classification and sequence classification with the same backbone.

Alternatives such as IndicBERT or MuRIL are also reasonable; mBERT was chosen for availability and because it is the model used in the course notes. The guide's design-decisions chapter discusses the trade-offs.

Check yourself. Why does the ordering model use the [CLS] vector rather than averaging all token vectors? The [CLS] representation is trained end-to-end for the pair classification objective, so it learns which aspects of the two sentences matter for their relative order. Averaging is a fixed, weaker pooling operation.

9. Transfer learning, fine-tuning, and LoRA

APPROACH CHOSEN. Freeze the mBERT backbone and train LoRA adapters on the query and value projections, plus the full classification head. This is the single most important memory decision in the project. It gives two task-specific models for roughly the cost of one backbone, and the adapters are small enough to save and reload.

9.1 Pretraining and fine-tuning

Pretraining teaches a model general language structure on a large corpus. Fine-tuning continues training on a smaller, task-specific dataset. The pretrained weights give the model a strong starting point, so fine-tuning needs much less data and time than training from scratch.

Full fine-tuning updates every weight. For a 178M-parameter model, that means storing gradients for all 178M parameters, storing optimizer state (AdamW keeps two moments per parameter), and accepting a large memory footprint and a risk of catastrophic forgetting.

The assignment's 1.5 GB budget makes full fine-tuning unattractive.

9.2 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) freezes most of the pretrained model and trains a small number of new parameters. The most popular method is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation).

9.3 The LoRA idea

A linear layer computes y = Wx. Full fine-tuning learns a new W'. LoRA instead freezes W and learns a low-rank update:

W' = W + (alpha / r) * B A

where:

  • W is the frozen pretrained matrix, shape d_out x d_in,
  • A is a small matrix of shape r x d_in,
  • B is a small matrix of shape d_out x r,
  • r is the rank (16 in this project),
  • alpha is a scaling constant (32 here).

The number of trainable parameters per adapted matrix is r(d_in + d_out), which is tiny compared with d_in * d_out.

For mBERT, d = 768 and r = 16:

per matrix = 16 * (768 + 768) = 24,576

The project adapts the query and value projections in all 12 layers. That is 24 matrices:

24 * 24,576 = 589,824

Add the fully trained classification head:

  • punctuation: 768 * 5 + 5 = 3,845
  • ordering: 768 * 2 + 2 = 1,538

So:

Stage LoRA params Head params Trainable total Total model Trainable fraction
Punctuation 589,824 3,845 593,669 177,860,362 0.334%
Ordering 589,824 1,538 591,362 about 178.4M 0.331%

Why query and value? Adapting Q and V changes what each token looks for and what it passes on, which is a strong lever for representation learning. Adapting the output projection is also common; adapting every matrix costs more. The choice is a standard, effective default.

9.4 LoRA hyperparameters

Parameter Value Meaning
r 16 rank of the update; higher = more capacity, more parameters
lora_alpha 32 scaling; effective scale is alpha/r = 2
lora_dropout 0.1 dropout on the LoRA path
target_modules ['query', 'value'] which linear layers get adapters

The adapter files are tiny. adapter_model.safetensors contains the LoRA matrices and the classifier head; the base model is loaded separately. At inference time, PeftModel.from_pretrained(base_model, adapter_dir) reconstructs the adapted model.

Important nuance. LoRA does not reduce the memory needed to store the base model. It reduces the memory needed to train. At inference, the base weights are still there. The project's savings come from fp16 and from loading one model at a time, not from LoRA alone.

9.5 Adapter merging

LoRA updates can be merged into the base weights for deployment: W' = W + (alpha/r) B A. After merging, inference has no LoRA overhead. This project keeps the adapter separate for clarity and because the merged base would be large. IndicTrans2, by contrast, is used as a full model (no LoRA).

Check yourself. If LoRA rank is increased from 16 to 64, what happens to the number of trainable parameters and to overfitting risk? Parameters grow roughly linearly in r (4x), capacity grows, and overfitting risk grows, especially with only 4,000 training examples.

10. Training mechanics

APPROACH CHOSEN. Train each stage independently with a standard Hugging Face `Trainer`, a fixed seed, article-level splits, and a task-appropriate validation metric. Punctuation uses class weights and boundary F1; ordering uses balanced pairs and accuracy. Everything else is deliberately conventional so the unusual parts (LoRA, staging, Devanagari handling) stand out.

10.1 Data splits

The project splits by article, not by window or sentence:

  • 2,700 articles for training,
  • 150 for validation,
  • 150 for test.

Splitting by article prevents leakage: two windows from the same article, or two sentence pairs from the same article, can be highly correlated. If one appeared in training and the other in test, the test score would be optimistic. The same article IDs define both the punctuation split and the ordering split, so the two stages are evaluated on the same held-out articles.

10.2 Batch, step, epoch

  • Batch size is the number of examples processed before one optimizer update.
  • Step is one optimizer update.
  • Epoch is one full pass over the training set.

With 25,000 windows, batch size 8, and gradient accumulation 2, one epoch has about ceil(25,000 / (8 * 2)) = 1,563 optimizer steps. The effective batch size is 16 examples per update.

10.3 Learning rate, warmup, weight decay

  • Learning rate (LR) controls step size. Too high diverges; too low learns slowly.
  • Warmup gradually increases the LR from near zero to the target over the first 6% of steps, which stabilizes early training.
  • Weight decay penalizes large weights (a regularizer). The project uses 0.01.
  • AdamW is the optimizer: Adam with decoupled weight decay. It maintains a moving average of gradients and squared gradients per parameter.

10.4 Gradient accumulation and checkpointing

  • Gradient accumulation sums gradients over several mini-batches before stepping. It simulates a larger batch without the memory cost.
  • Gradient checkpointing stores fewer activations during the forward pass and recomputes them during the backward pass. It trades compute for memory. This is essential for fitting mBERT in a small budget.

10.5 Mixed precision

fp16 stores activations and weights in 16-bit floats, roughly halving memory and speeding up math on supported GPUs. The project enables fp16 only on CUDA:

fp16=torch.cuda.is_available()

On Apple MPS, training runs in fp32 because this torch build's fp16/MPS path is not reliable. Inference uses fp16 on CUDA and fp32 otherwise.

10.6 Seeds and reproducibility

set_seed(42) seeds Python, NumPy, and PyTorch. The data preparation uses seeded random.Random instances for shuffling and splitting. Exact reproducibility across hardware is never guaranteed (different GPUs, different kernels, nondeterministic operations), but the same machine and seed give stable results.

10.7 Class weights

The punctuation dataset is extremely imbalanced: 90.9% of words take no mark. A plain cross-entropy loss would learn to predict none almost always and still achieve high accuracy. The project uses inverse-frequency class weights:

w_c = N / (K * n_c)

where N is the total number of labels, K = 5 is the number of classes, and n_c is the count of class c. Weights are capped at 20 to avoid instability.

Using the training counts:

10.10 Diagnosing training

Signals to watch:

  • Loss goes down, validation F1 goes up: learning.
  • Training loss goes down, validation loss goes up: overfitting; reduce epochs, increase dropout, add data.
  • Both losses flat: learning rate too low, or labels misaligned.
  • Loss becomes NaN: learning rate too high, fp16 instability, or bad class weights.
  • Accuracy high but F1 low: class imbalance; look at per-class scores.
  • Boundary F1 high but comma F1 low: the model is good at sentence endings but weak on commas; inspect comma examples.

The local training summaries show healthy learning: punctuation eval loss 0.4399, boundary F1 0.8246 after 2 epochs; ordering eval loss 0.5827, macro F1 0.6768 after 1 epoch. The ordering model is not overfit after one epoch, which is why the full recipe uses two.

11. Inference and decoding

APPROACH CHOSEN. Keep the inference path as close as possible to the training distribution, then add only the minimum decoding logic needed. Punctuation uses overlapping windows with triangular weighting; ordering uses an explicit global decoder; translation uses standard beam search. No stage relies on an LLM or a hidden heuristic.

11.1 Argmax and softmax

For a classification model, the simplest decoder is argmax: choose the class with the highest logit. Equivalently, choose the highest softmax probability. The punctuation model uses argmax after averaging probabilities across windows.

11.2 Sliding windows and triangular weighting

A document can be longer than the model's maximum input length. The punctuation model uses 64-word windows with stride 40. A word near the edge of a window has less context on one side, so its prediction is less reliable. The project averages predictions with a triangular weight:

w(p, L) = max(0.05, 1 - |p - c| / (c + 1)),  c = (L - 1) / 2

where p is the position in the window and L is the window length. Words in the middle get weight near 1; words at the edges get weight near 0.05. Predictions are accumulated as score[word] += weight * probability and divided by the total weight.

Why this works: every word is seen in multiple windows (except near document edges), and the windows where it is central dominate. The result is smoother and more accurate than using a single window.

11.3 Batching

Inference processes windows in batches (16 for punctuation, 32 pairs for ordering, 4 sentences for translation). Batching improves GPU utilization. The code sorts nothing; it simply chunks the list.

11.4 Beam search

For sequence generation, greedy decoding picks the most likely next token at each step. Beam search keeps the k most likely partial sequences (the beam) and expands them, which can find a better global sequence. IndicTrans2 uses num_beams=4, max_length=256, and early_stopping=True.

Beam search is not used for punctuation or pair classification because those are single-step decisions, not sequences.

11.5 Ordering decoders

The ordering model produces a matrix M where M[i,j] = P(sentence i before j). Three decoders turn this matrix into a permutation:

  • Borda (default): score each sentence by its mean probability of coming before all others, then sort descending.
  • Beam: build a chain, keeping the best partial chains, scoring each adjacent pair with M.
  • Clusters: TF-IDF + KMeans to group sentences, order cluster medoids with the pair model, then beam-search inside each cluster.

The evaluation compares all three plus a TF-IDF-only ablation. Borda wins on mean Kendall's tau.

11.6 Complexity

For n sentences, the pair model evaluates n(n-1)/2 pairs. For 99 sentences that is 4,851 pairs. This quadratic cost is why ordering is the slowest stage in the pipeline (130.53 seconds for all seven files on MPS). Clustering reduces the number of expensive pairs inside clusters, but the project still computes the full matrix for evaluation.

Check yourself. Why is M[j,i] = 1 - M[i,j] valid? The classifier is trained on both orders of every pair, so it approximates a symmetric preference. The code explicitly fills the transpose as the complement to keep the matrix consistent and avoid a second model call.

12. Metrics: what the numbers mean

APPROACH CHOSEN. Report the metric that matches the decision being made, not a single headline number. Boundary F1 for punctuation (not accuracy), pair F1 plus Kendall's tau for ordering (not pair accuracy alone), and BLEU plus chrF for translation (not one metric).

12.1 Confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, F1

For binary decisions, define:

  • True positive (TP): predicted positive, actually positive.
  • False positive (FP): predicted positive, actually negative.
  • True negative (TN): predicted negative, actually negative.
  • False negative (FN): predicted negative, actually positive.

Then:

accuracy  = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)
precision = TP / (TP + FP)
recall    = TP / (TP + FN)
F1        = 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall)

Accuracy is intuitive but misleading under imbalance. Precision asks “when the model says positive, how often is it right?” Recall asks “of all real positives, how many did the model find?” F1 balances the two.

12.2 Macro, micro, weighted

  • Macro average: compute the metric per class and average, giving each class equal weight. Good when rare classes matter.
  • Micro average: pool all decisions and compute globally; dominated by frequent classes.
  • Weighted average: average per-class metrics weighted by class support.

The ordering pair metrics use macro averaging because the two classes are balanced but we still want a symmetric view. The punctuation per-class F1 is reported class by class, which is more informative than any single average.

12.3 Boundary F1

The punctuation model predicts five labels. For the assignment, what matters most is whether a word is a sentence boundary. Define:

  • boundary = label in {sentence_end, question, exclamation}.
  • non-boundary = {none, comma}.

Then boundary precision/recall/F1 treat boundary detection as a binary problem. This collapses the rare question/exclamation classes into the boundary class, which is useful because those classes are too rare to evaluate separately. It still does not fix the fact that the model rarely predicts them.

12.6 Kendall's tau

Kendall's tau measures rank correlation between the recovered order and the true order. For every pair of items, count whether their relative order agrees or disagrees:

tau = (concordant - discordant) / (concordant + discordant)
  • tau = 1: perfect order.
  • tau = 0: no better than random.
  • tau = -1: exactly reversed.

The evaluation shuffles each held-out article with a fixed seed, recovers an order, and computes tau against the original order. Results on 40 articles:

Borda is the best on average, which justifies making it the default. Some articles have negative tau, meaning the recovered order is worse than random for that article. This happens when the pairwise model is confidently wrong about a topic shift. Max tau around 0.64-0.71 shows that some articles are recovered reasonably well.

12.7 BLEU

BLEU measures n-gram overlap between a hypothesis translation and one or more references, with a brevity penalty:

BLEU = BP * exp(sum_{n=1..N} w_n * log(p_n))
  • p_n is the clipped n-gram precision: the fraction of hypothesis n-grams that appear in the reference, with counts clipped to the reference count.
  • BP = min(1, exp(1 - r/c)) where r is reference length and c is hypothesis length; it penalizes short outputs.
  • Usually N = 4 and weights are uniform.

BLEU rewards exact phrasing and is sensitive to word order. It correlates imperfectly with human judgment, especially for paraphrases.

12.8 chrF

chrF computes character n-gram precision and recall and combines them with an F-score. It is more tolerant of morphology and paraphrase than BLEU, which is useful for Hindi-English. The project uses CHRF(word_order=2), which also considers word bigrams.

14. The end-to-end mental model

Before diving into code, hold this picture in your head:

  1. Words in, labels out. Stage 1 does not rewrite words. It attaches one of five labels to each word. The words are preserved exactly (after NULL removal).
  2. Labels define sentences. A boundary label closes a sentence. The sentence text is the words plus their predicted marks.
  3. Sentences are nodes. Stage 2 treats each sentence as a node and asks a pairwise question about every pair. The output is a matrix of probabilities, not an order.
  4. A decoder turns the matrix into an order. This is a separate algorithmic step; the model does not directly output a permutation.
  5. Translation is sentence-level. Each ordered sentence is translated independently. Grouping happens after translation, by position.
  6. Memory is managed by staging. Load, use, free, repeat. The budget applies to the largest stage.
  7. Every stage has a metric. Token/boundary F1 for punctuation, pair F1 and Kendall's tau for ordering, BLEU/chrF for translation.

If you can explain these seven points and the data shapes, you can explain the project.

17. Data preparation deep dive

APPROACH CHOSEN. Create supervision by hiding information that Wikipedia already contains. Punctuation is removed and stored as word labels; sentence order is shuffled inside windows; ordering pairs come from consecutive sentences. Split by article, cap examples for tractability, and never synthesize labels with an LLM.

src/data_prep.py turns raw Wikipedia into two datasets. It is worth understanding line by line because the model can only be as good as its labels.

17.1 Streaming Wikipedia

stream = load_dataset(WIKI_DATASET, WIKI_CONFIG, split='train', streaming=True)
  • wikimedia/wikipedia with config 20231101.hi is the November 2023 Hindi Wikipedia dump.
  • streaming=True means rows are downloaded on demand; the full dump is never stored.
  • scan_multiplier=3 means the loader scans up to 9,000 pages to keep 3,000 usable articles. Some pages are lists, stubs, or otherwise unusable.

17.2 Parsing an article

ARTICLE_PATTERN = re.compile(r'(?<=[।॥?!])\s+')

Steps in parse_article:

  1. Remove citation markers like [12] with a regex.
  2. Collapse whitespace.
  3. Split after sentence-final marks using the lookbehind regex.
  4. For each token, call strip_punctuation() to separate the word from its trailing mark.
  5. Keep tokens that contain Devanagari or a digit. This drops stray Latin words, URLs, and markup.
  6. Accumulate words until a boundary label. If the sentence length is between 5 and 40 words, keep it. Otherwise discard it.
  7. If the article yields fewer than 3 sentences, discard the article.

Why discard short sentences? Wikipedia has many fragments (headings, list items, captions). Training on them would teach the model that a sentence can be two words long, which is not true for the assignment's prose. The 5-word minimum is a data-cleaning choice, and it also explains why dialogue-like samples are over-segmented at inference.

17.3 Punctuation windows

make_punctuation_windows slides over the article's sentences:

  • Start at sentence index.
  • Add sentences until the total would exceed 96 words or reaches at least 64 words.
  • Keep the window if it has 32-96 words and at least 2 sentences.
  • Shuffle the sentences inside the window with the seeded RNG.
  • Concatenate their words and labels.
  • Advance by at least 40 words (stride), moving whole sentences.

Why shuffle? The assignment input has shuffled sentences. If training windows kept the original order, the model could learn cross-sentence continuity cues that will not exist at inference. Shuffling forces the model to rely on local word context, which is the right signal for punctuation.

The window is measured in words, not subwords. A 64-word window may become 100+ subword tokens; MAX_SEQ_LENGTH=128 truncates the tail. The first-subword label alignment ensures the remaining words still have labels.

17.4 Splitting by article

Article IDs are shuffled with a fixed seed, then split 90/5/5. The same split is used for punctuation and ordering. This prevents leakage and makes the two stages comparable.

17.5 Capping examples

Training punctuation is capped at 25,000 windows, ordering at 40,000 pairs. Evaluation is capped at 3,000 pairs (and the punctuation test is used in full unless --max-punct-examples is set). The cap keeps training time bounded and makes runs comparable.

Subtle detail. The punctuation loop breaks out of the article loop once it has 1.2x the window limit, then shuffles and caps. This means the training set is a random sample of the first ~30,000 windows, not a random sample of all possible windows. For a course project this is fine; for a production system you would stream all articles and sample uniformly.

17.6 Ordering pairs

src/ordering/pairs.py:13-49 builds pairs from consecutive sentences:

  • Choose up to 30 positions per article, evenly spaced if the article is longer.
  • For each position, take (sentences[i], sentences[i+1]).
  • Emit a positive example (A, B, label=1).
  • Emit a negative example (B, A, label=0).

This gives a balanced binary dataset and teaches the model the direction of local narrative flow. It does not teach global order directly; the decoder must combine local preferences into a global permutation.

17.7 The held-out ordering articles

For Kendall's tau, the project saves 40 test articles with 8-70 sentences in their original order:

{'article_id': 'hi-01234', 'sentences': ['...', '...', '...']}

At evaluation time, each article is shuffled with a fixed seed, the model scores all pairs, each decoder recovers an order, and tau is computed against the original indices. This measures full-order recovery, not just pair accuracy.

18. Stage 1: Punctuation restoration

APPROACH CHOSEN. Treat punctuation as word-level token classification. Label each word with one of five marks, align the label to the first subword, use a class-weighted loss, and reconstruct sentences from boundary labels. This is the simplest formulation that preserves the input words and fits the memory budget.

18.1 Problem formulation

Input: a list of Hindi words [w_1, ..., w_n] with no punctuation. Output: a label y_i for each word, where y_i is one of:

Label Mark Meaning
none '' no punctuation after this word
comma , comma after this word
sentence_end । sentence ends here
question ? question ends here
exclamation ! exclamation ends here

The label belongs to the word before the mark. For example, in हुआ।, the word is हुआ and the label is sentence_end.

18.2 Why token classification

Alternatives:

  • Sequence-to-sequence rewriting: generate the punctuated text. More flexible, but can change words, is slower, and is harder to evaluate at the word level.
  • Boundary-only binary classification: predict boundary vs non-boundary, then a second model chooses the mark. Simpler, but loses the joint information and needs two models.
  • Token classification: one label per word, words preserved, single model. This is the chosen approach.

18.3 Subword alignment

The dataset code (src/punctuation/dataset.py) does:

encoding = tokenizer(row['words'], is_split_into_words=True,
                     truncation=True, max_length=128)
word_ids = encoding.word_ids()
labels = [-100] * len(encoding['input_ids'])
previous_word = None
for position, word_id in enumerate(word_ids):
    if word_id is None or word_id == previous_word:
        continue
    previous_word = word_id
    labels[position] = LABEL2ID[row['labels'][word_id]]

For अयोध्या (pieces अ, ##य, ##ोध, ##्या), the label goes on अ; the three continuation pieces get -100. At inference, the code reads the prediction at the first subword of each word.

18.4 Model and head

AutoModelForTokenClassification.from_pretrained(BASE_MODEL, num_labels=5, ...) loads mBERT and adds a linear head. The head maps each 768-dimensional hidden state to 5 logits. The LoRA adapters are attached to the query and value projections.

18.5 Class-weighted loss

WeightedTrainer overrides compute_loss:

loss_function = torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss(
    weight=self.class_weight_tensor.to(logits.device),
    ignore_index=-100,
)
loss = loss_function(logits.view(-1, logits.size(-1)), labels.view(-1))

This is the only non-standard training component. It is necessary because question and exclamation marks are almost absent.

18.6 Inference: sliding windows and triangular weights

PunctuationRestorer.predict_labels:

  1. Compute windows of 64 words with stride 40.
  2. Tokenize each window with is_split_into_words=True, max_length=160.
  3. Run the model in batches of 16.
  4. Softmax the logits.
  5. For each word, take the first subword's probability vector.
  6. Weight it by window_weight(word_id, window_length).
  7. Accumulate and normalize.
  8. Argmax per word.

The triangular weight is the key smoothing trick. Without it, a word at the edge of one window might get a bad prediction that overrides a good prediction from another window.

18.7 Rebuilding sentences

split_into_sentences(words, labels, with_punctuation=True) walks the words:

  • append word + PUNCTUATION[label],
  • if the label is a boundary, close the current sentence.

with_punctuation=False gives plain sentences for the ordering model, which was trained on plain text (no punctuation). This is important: the ordering model should see the same distribution it was trained on.

18.8 What the model learns

The model learns local cues:

  • a danda or period after a finite verb,
  • a comma after a clause or list item,
  • sentence endings after particles like है, था, हैं, गया,
  • question marks after question words or interrogative particles (rarely, because of data scarcity).

It does not learn global discourse structure; that is the ordering stage's job.

18.10 Known limitations

  • Comma F1 is low (0.3161). Commas are context-dependent and less frequent than sentence endings. The model often misses them.
  • Question/exclamation F1 is 0. There are only 236 and 153 training examples, and the cap on class weights (20) limits their influence. The model essentially never predicts them.
  • Over-segmentation on dialogue. Short utterances look like sentence endings. Samples 6 and 7 show this.
  • Truncation. A 64-word window can exceed 128 subwords; the tail is truncated. This affects long words and complex compounds.
  • Danda vs period. The model predicts sentence_end, which is rendered as । regardless of whether the original used .. For Hindi output this is appropriate.

Check yourself. Why is the comma the hardest class? Because comma placement depends on clause structure and style, not just local word identity. A comma can be optional in many positions, so the label is inherently ambiguous. Sentence endings have stronger cues (finite verbs, particles).

19. Stage 2: Sentence ordering

APPROACH CHOSEN. Learn only pairwise direction, then decode globally. A binary mBERT classifier answers “does A come before B?” for every pair; Borda aggregation turns the probability matrix into a full order. We compare Borda against beam and clustering decoders, and against a TF-IDF-only ablation, rather than assuming one is best.

19.1 Problem formulation

Input: a list of sentences in random order. Output: a permutation that recovers the original narrative order.

The project decomposes this into pairwise decisions: for sentences A and B, predict whether A comes before B. Then a decoder aggregates the pairwise probabilities into a global order.

19.2 Why pairwise

Alternatives:

  • Direct permutation prediction: a model outputs a permutation of n items. Hard to train, variable output size, and expensive.
  • Pointer networks: generate the order one sentence at a time, attending to the remaining sentences. Elegant, but more complex and harder to fit in the budget.
  • Pairwise ranking: train a binary classifier on pairs, then decode. Simple, generalizes to any n, and the pairwise model can be reused by multiple decoders.

The pairwise approach is the standard compromise and matches the course notes.

19.3 Building balanced pairs

For consecutive sentences in a Wikipedia article:

(A, B, 1)   # A really comes before B
(B, A, 0)   # the reverse is wrong

This gives a 50/50 label distribution, so accuracy is meaningful and no class weighting is needed. The model sees both directions of the same pair, which also teaches symmetry.

19.4 Model

AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(BASE_MODEL, num_labels=2) loads mBERT and adds a linear head on the [CLS] vector. The input format is:

[CLS] sentence A [SEP] sentence B [SEP]

The tokenizer creates token type IDs (segment IDs): 0 for A, 1 for B. BERT's embeddings add a segment embedding, so the model knows which sentence is which. The [CLS] representation is passed to the classifier, which outputs two logits.

19.5 Inference: the score matrix

OrderScorer.score_matrix(sentences):

  1. Enumerate all i < j pairs.
  2. Batch them through the model.
  3. For each pair, take softmax(logits)[1] = P(A before B).
  4. Fill M[i,j] = p, M[j,i] = 1 - p.
  5. Diagonal stays 0.5.

The matrix is symmetric-complement, so every decoder can look up either direction.

19.6 Decoder 1: Borda

For each sentence i:

s_i = (1 / (n - 1)) * sum_{j != i} M[i, j]

Sort sentences by s_i descending. A sentence that tends to come before others gets a high score and moves to the front.

Why it works: averaging over all pairs reduces the impact of individual pair errors. It is a Borda/Kemeny-style approximation. The true Kemeny order minimizes the number of pairwise disagreements and is NP-hard; Borda is a cheap approximation.

19.7 Decoder 2: clusters

  1. TF-IDF with analyzer='char_wb', ngram_range=(2,4), sublinear_tf=True.
  2. KMeans with k = round(n/6), clamped to 1-12.
  3. For each cluster, choose the medoid: the member with the highest cosine similarity to the cluster centroid.
  4. Order the medoids with a greedy pass over the medoid submatrix.
  5. Beam-search inside each cluster.
  6. Concatenate cluster orders.

Why cluster? Narrative order often moves between topics. Grouping topically similar sentences reduces the number of expensive pair comparisons and can improve local coherence. In the evaluation, clusters underperform Borda on average, but it is a useful comparison and follows the notes closely.

19.8 Decoder 3: beam

Beam search over chains:

  • Start from the beam_width sentences with the highest earliness score.
  • Expand each partial chain by appending a remaining sentence.
  • Score the chain by the sum of adjacent pair probabilities.
  • Keep the top beam_width chains.
  • Return the best full chain.

Beam is greedy in the sense that it only scores adjacent transitions, so an early mistake can propagate. It performs worst of the three on average.

19.9 TF-IDF ablation

A pure lexical baseline:

  • TF-IDF char 2-4 grams,
  • cosine similarity matrix,
  • diagonal zeroed,
  • greedy order by similarity to the current sentence.

This measures how much the mBERT pair model adds over simple lexical cohesion. The answer: a lot, but not enormous. Borda tau 0.2007 vs TF-IDF 0.0631.

19.10 Kendall's tau evaluation

For each of 40 held-out articles:

  1. Start from the true order [0, 1, ..., n-1].
  2. Shuffle with a fixed seed to get shuffled_indices.
  3. Score the shuffled sentences.
  4. Decode to positions in the shuffled list.
  5. Map back to original indices.
  6. Compute kendalltau(recovered, truth).

One matrix is computed per article and reused by all decoders, so comparing them is cheap.

19.12 Known limitations

  • Weak global tau (0.2007). Pairwise accuracy 65% does not translate into strong full-order recovery.
  • Topical vs narrative order. The model learns local coherence, but Wikipedia articles often move between topics in ways that are not predictable from adjacent sentences.
  • Long articles. Quadratic pair count and truncation at 192 tokens. For 99 sentences, the model makes 4,851 calls.
  • No global constraints. Borda can produce an order that violates transitivity (A before B, B before C, but C before A in the scores). The decoder does not enforce a consistent global ranking.
  • Training data size. The local adapter saw only 4,000 pairs. The full recipe uses 40,000.

Check yourself. Why can a model with 65% pair accuracy produce an order with tau close to zero? Because errors are correlated and early mistakes propagate. Global order requires getting many pairs right simultaneously, and a few confidently wrong pairs near topic boundaries can scramble large blocks.

20.2 IndicProcessor

IndicTrans2 expects language tags and preprocessing. IndicProcessor(inference=True):

  • adds the source/target tags (hin_Deva, eng_Latn),
  • normalizes and tokenizes text according to the model's conventions,
  • postprocesses generated text.

The wrapper calls:

prepared = self.processor.preprocess_batch(batch, src_lang='hin_Deva',
                                           tgt_lang='eng_Latn')

Then tokenizes and generates.

Subtle bug fixed during development. The tokenizer is called without src_lang/tgt_lang kwargs because the processor already inserted the tags. Passing them again causes errors. The wrapper also sets return_attention_mask=True and clean_up_tokenization_spaces=True, and supplies config.vocab_size from the decoder vocabulary when the config lacks a top-level vocab_size.

20.3 Generation

generated = self.model.generate(
    **encoded,
    num_beams=4,
    num_return_sequences=1,
    max_length=256,
    early_stopping=True,
)
  • Beam search (4 beams): explores four partial translations, which usually beats greedy decoding.
  • Max length 256: enough for the assignment's sentences; longer inputs are truncated.
  • Early stopping: stop when all beams are finished.

The output is decoded with skip_special_tokens=True, then postprocessed by IndicProcessor.

20.4 Batching and memory

  • Batch size defaults to 4 in the pipeline and can be raised with --translation-batch-size.
  • On CUDA, dtype is fp16; on MPS/CPU, fp32.
  • The model is loaded with low_cpu_mem_usage=True.
  • After translation, close() deletes the model and calls free_memory().

20.6 Why not a larger model or an LLM

  • The assignment forbids LLMs.
  • A 200M model fits the 1.5 GB budget; a 7B model does not.
  • IndicTrans2 is specialized for Indic languages and outperforms generic small models on this task.
  • A dedicated translation model is easier to evaluate and reproduce than a prompted LLM.

20.7 Known limitations

  • Gated access. The official model requires authentication; the local mirror is a workaround.
  • Sentence-level translation. Each sentence is translated independently, so cross-sentence discourse is lost.
  • Truncation. Sentences longer than 256 tokens are cut.
  • Paraphrase. BLEU penalizes valid paraphrases; chrF is more forgiving.
  • No glossary control. Domain terms may be translated inconsistently.
  • Local mirror provenance. The local merged mirror should be replaced with the official checkpoint for a final submission if possible.

Check yourself. Why translate sentence by sentence instead of translating the whole document at once? Because the assignment requires sentence-aligned Hindi/English outputs and groups of five. Sentence-level translation keeps alignment exact. Document-level translation might improve coherence but would require alignment heuristics and could exceed the model's context.

21. Pipeline orchestration

APPROACH CHOSEN. Orchestrate with a thin, explicit script rather than a framework. Load all text first, then run stage 1 on every file, free it, run stage 2 on every file, free it, run stage 3 on all sentences, and write aligned outputs. The code is short enough to audit and makes the memory story obvious.

src/pipeline.py is the glue. It is short, and every line matters.

21.1 Reading tokens

def read_tokens(path):
    text = Path(path).read_text(encoding='utf-8')
    raw_tokens = text.split()
    words = [token for token in raw_tokens if token != 'NULL']
    return words, len(raw_tokens) - len(words)
  • str.split() handles arbitrary whitespace and preserves Devanagari.
  • NULL tokens are removed. The count is recorded for the report.
  • All records are loaded before any model runs, so the memory used by raw text is negligible.

21.2 Stage 1

restorer = PunctuationRestorer(args.punctuation_model, memory_gb=args.memory_gb)
for stem, record in records.items():
    labels, sentences, plain_sentences = restorer.restore(record['words'])
    ...
restorer.close()

The restorer returns three things:

  • labels: one label ID per word,
  • sentences: punctuated Hindi sentences,
  • plain_sentences: the same sentences without punctuation, for the ordering model.

21.3 Stage 2

scorer = OrderScorer(args.ordering_model, memory_gb=args.memory_gb)
for stem, record in records.items():
    order = order_sentences(record['sentences_plain'], scorer,
                            strategy=args.strategy, ...)
    record['ordered_hi'] = [record['sentences_hi'][i] for i in order]
    record['groups_hi'] = group_lines(record['ordered_hi'], GROUP_SIZE)
scorer.close()

Note the index mapping: the order is computed on plain sentences, but applied to the punctuated sentences. The two lists have the same length and the same sentence boundaries, so the mapping is valid. This is a small but important detail: the ordering model sees the text distribution it was trained on, while the output keeps punctuation.

21.4 Stage 3

flat_sentences = [s for record in records.values() for s in record['ordered_hi']]
translations = translator.translate(flat_sentences,
                                    batch_size=args.translation_batch_size)

All sentences from all files are flattened into one list and translated in batches. Then the translations are sliced back per file using the sentence counts. This maximizes batch utilization.

Grouping:

record['groups_en'] = [
    ' '.join(group)
    for group in group_lines(record['ordered_en'], GROUP_SIZE)
]

The Hindi groups are built from sentences, the English groups from translations, and both use the same GROUP_SIZE=5, so they stay aligned.

21.5 Saving

For each file, four outputs are written. The summary records words, NULL removed, sentence count, group count, and stage timings. It is saved to reports/run_summary.json.

21.6 Failure behavior

  • Missing punctuation adapter: FileNotFoundError with a message telling you to train it.
  • Missing ordering adapter: same.
  • Missing IndicTrans2 or toolkit: ImportError with a setup hint.
  • No input files: SystemExit with the directory path.
  • Translation failure: raises; there is no fallback model.

This is deliberate. Silent fallbacks make results hard to reproduce and can hide a broken setup.

22. Evaluation

APPROACH CHOSEN. Evaluate each stage on its own held-out data with the metric that matches the task, and write a single JSON report. 22.1 Punctuation evaluation

  • Load 1,500 test windows (108,898 words).
  • Run the full sliding-window inference (PunctuationRestorer.predict_labels).
  • Flatten true and predicted labels.
  • Compute token accuracy, boundary precision/recall/F1, and per-class F1.

  • Load 3,000 balanced test pairs.
  • Predict labels with OrderScorer.predict_pair_labels.
  • Compute accuracy and macro precision/recall/F1.
  • Load 40 held-out articles, shuffle each, score all pairs, decode with all strategies plus TF-IDF, and compute Kendall's tau.

  • Stream cfilt/iitb-english-hindi test split.
  • Take the first 100 (or --max-translation-examples) Hindi/English pairs.
  • Translate the Hindi with the same wrapper.
  • Compute corpus BLEU and chrF with sacrebleu.
  • Save five sample translations in the report.

lessons/train.py is a TF-IDF + logistic regression text classifier. It expects data/raw/dataset.csv with text and label columns, which is not part of the assignment data. It is an educational example.

26.2 Baseline

The baseline is a complete rule-based pipeline:

  • Segmentation: dynamic programming over candidate boundaries, scored by verb endings, sentence starters, NULL hints, and a length prior.
  • Ordering: TF-IDF char 2-4 grams + KMeans + greedy medoid ordering.
  • Translation: Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-hi-en MarianMT.

It works, but it is brittle: the segmentation rules are hand-tuned, the ordering is lexical, and the translation model is generic. The final pipeline replaces every heuristic with a trained model. The baseline remains useful as a comparison and as a record of the engineering path.

Check yourself. Why keep the baseline if it is not used? Because it documents the alternatives and provides a sanity check. If the trained pipeline were worse than a simple rule-based system, that would be important to know. It also shows the evolution of the solution, which is valuable in a project report.

27.2 Interpretation

Punctuation is mostly a sentence-end problem. The model is strong at deciding where a sentence ends. It is weak at commas, because comma placement is stylistic and context-dependent, and the training data has only 3.3% commas. Question and exclamation marks are effectively absent, so no model can learn them from this data. The high token accuracy is partly an artifact of the none majority: predicting none everywhere would already score about 0.91. Boundary F1 is the honest metric.

Ordering is better than lexical but not solved. Pair accuracy of 0.65 means the model has learned real coherence signals. But full-order recovery via Borda is weak (tau 0.20). Pairwise preferences are noisy and can conflict, and a wrong early decision propagates. Borda is the best of the three decoders; that is why it is the default.

Translation is the strongest stage. BLEU 34 on IITB is a reasonable result for a 200M-parameter Indic→English model on general prose. It is not a claim about this specific noisy, reordered input. Translation is also the least affected by the upstream stages on well-formed sentences, because IndicTrans2 is robust to imperfect punctuation.

27.3 Limitations, stated plainly

  1. Subset adapters. The shipped adapters were trained on 4,000 punctuation windows and 4,000 ordering pairs, not the full 25,000/40,000. The full recipe is documented, but full-data training was not completed locally.
  2. Synthetic supervision. Punctuation labels and ordering pairs come from Wikipedia text. The model learns Wikipedia style. Dialogue, poetry, and informal text behave differently.
  3. Rare labels. Question and exclamation marks are almost absent. Do not expect them.
  4. Ordering ceiling. With a 64-word window and pairwise BERT scoring, full-document order remains hard. The grouping step hides some errors by keeping groups of five in the same relative order, but it does not fix them.
  5. Translation evaluation. BLEU/chrF are computed on clean IITB sentence pairs, not on the pipeline's reordered, punctuated output. They measure the translator, not the full pipeline.
  6. Gated model. The official IndicTrans2 requires license acceptance. A local mirror was used. Results may differ slightly from the official checkpoint.
  7. No test suite. Verification is by scripts, reports, and the executed notebook, not by unit tests.
  8. Single seed. All results are from one run with SEED=42. Variance is not measured.

28. Design decisions, expanded

APPROACH CHOSEN. Make each major decision explicit, with the alternative, the reason, and the cost. This is the material a reviewer or examiner will probe, so it is written as a decision log rather than prose.

D1. Language: Hindi

  • Chosen: Hindi (Devanagari), with English translation.
  • Alternatives: a higher-resource language with better tooling.
  • Why: the task requires Hindi; Devanagari has clear sentence-ending danda, rich morphology, and available multilingual models.
  • Cost: fewer high-quality tools than English; tokenization is harder.

D2. Tokenization: whitespace, not regex

  • Chosen: str.split().
  • Alternatives: re.findall(r'\w+', text).
  • Why: Devanagari vowel signs are combining marks; \w+ drops them (अयोध्या → अय, ध, य). Whitespace keeps words intact.
  • Cost: punctuation attached to words must be handled by the models or stripped.

D3. Punctuation: token classification, not seq2seq

  • Chosen: classify each word into five labels.
  • Alternatives: generate punctuated text with a seq2seq model; use a large LLM.
  • Why: one-to-one alignment, fast, small, no hallucination, fits 1.5 GB.
  • Cost: cannot insert words; rare labels are hard; comma style is limited.

D4. Model: mBERT + LoRA

  • Chosen: bert-base-multilingual-cased with LoRA r=16 on query/value.
  • Alternatives: full fine-tuning; a larger multilingual model; XLM-R; MuRIL.
  • Why: mBERT has Hindi coverage, is ~178M params, and LoRA makes training cheap. It was available locally.
  • Cost: mBERT is older than XLM-R/MuRIL; cased vocab splits Devanagari heavily.

D5. Label alignment: first subword

  • Chosen: assign the word label to its first subword; ignore the rest.
  • Alternatives: average subword logits; predict on the last subword; use word-level tokenizer.
  • Why: Bengali/Hindi word endings carry punctuation cues, but first-subword is simple, stable, and works well in practice.
  • Cost: loses some information from later subwords; a learned pooling could be better.

D6. Ordering: pairwise binary classification + Borda

  • Chosen: train [CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP] → before/after; decode with Borda.
  • Alternatives: pointer networks; seq2seq ordering; listwise ranking; topological sort.
  • Why: pairwise data is easy to generate and the classifier is small and robust. Borda aggregates noisy preferences.
  • Cost: pairwise preferences can be cyclic; Borda is not optimal; full order is weak.

D7. Ordering training data: balanced both directions

  • Chosen: for each adjacent pair, add (A,B,1) and (B,A,0).
  • Alternatives: only natural order; sample random pairs.
  • Why: removes position bias and doubles data.
  • Cost: model cannot use absolute position; only relative coherence.

D8. Translation: IndicTrans2 200M

  • Chosen: ai4bharat/indictrans2-indic-en-dist-200M.
  • Alternatives: MarianMT; NLLB; a large LLM; Google Translate API.
  • Why: Indic-specialized, 200M is feasible, strong Hindi→English quality, runs locally.
  • Cost: gated license; ~913 MB; 256-token limit; slower on CPU.

D9. Memory: staged load/free vs simultaneous

  • Chosen: load one model, run its stage on all files, free it, load the next.
  • Alternatives: load all three and stream.
  • Why: three models would exceed 1.5 GB; staging caps peak memory at the largest model.
  • Cost: cannot jointly optimize stages; more I/O.

D10. Evaluation: per-stage, honest subsets

  • Chosen: held-out windows/pairs/articles and a 100-sentence translation sample.
  • Alternatives: one end-to-end human evaluation.
  • Why: fast, reproducible, isolates failures.
  • Cost: does not measure full-pipeline quality directly.

D11. Notebooks: recipe + executed demo

  • Chosen: a full Colab recipe notebook and an executed local demo.
  • Alternatives: only scripts; only a notebook.
  • Why: the recipe is portable; the demo is evidence.
  • Cost: two artifacts to maintain; the recipe is not executed here.

D12. No LLM

  • Chosen: no generative LLM anywhere in the pipeline.
  • Alternatives: prompt GPT/Gemini for punctuation/ordering/translation.
  • Why: the assignment forbids an LLM-based solution; also cost, latency, privacy, and determinism.
  • Cost: lower ceiling on quality, especially ordering.

29. Viva and interview question bank

APPROACH CHOSEN. Prepare short, defensible answers that connect each design choice to a constraint and a measurement. If you can answer the “why not” questions, you understand the project.

29.1 Problem and data

  1. What is the task? Restore punctuation in unpunctuated Hindi, reorder shuffled sentences into a coherent document, and translate to English.
  2. Why is Hindi hard? Rich morphology, free word order, and combining marks in Devanagari.
  3. Where does the data come from? 3,000 Hindi Wikipedia articles, split 2,700/150/150.
  4. How are punctuation labels derived? From the original punctuation attached to tokens; none/comma/sentence_end/question/exclamation.
  5. How are windows built? 64-word windows, stride 40, kept only if 32-96 words and 5-40-word sentences.
  6. Why stride 40? It gives overlap so boundary words appear in multiple windows and get averaged.
  7. How are ordering pairs built? Adjacent sentence pairs, both directions, balanced labels.
  8. Why balanced directions? To remove position bias and double the data.
  9. Why hold out articles for ordering? Pairs from the same article would leak context into evaluation.
  10. What is the NULL token? A placeholder in the raw data; it is removed before processing and counted.

29.2 Punctuation

  1. Why token classification? It preserves alignment and is fast, small, and deterministic.
  2. Why not seq2seq? It can hallucinate or change words, and alignment is harder.
  3. How do you handle subwords? Use the first subword's prediction for the word.
  4. Why first subword? Simple and stable; a learned pooling is future work.
  5. Why class weights? none is ~91%; weights [0.22, 6.13, 3.47, 20, 20] counter imbalance.
  6. Why cap weights at 20? Uncapped question would be ~1540, destabilizing training.
  7. How does sliding-window inference work? Predict per window, accumulate logits per word, average, argmax.
  8. Why window_weight? It counts how many windows scored each word so averaging is correct.
  9. What are boundary labels? sentence_end, question, exclamation; none and comma are not boundaries.
  10. Why is comma F1 low? Commas are stylistic, sparse, and context-dependent.
  11. Why are question/exclamation F1 zero? Only 236/153 training examples; the model never predicts them.
  12. What is restore_punctuation? A lightweight function that attaches punctuation for display, capped at one terminal mark.
  13. Why not predict punctuation for every word independently in the pipeline? That is exactly what it does; the labels come from averaged window logits.
  14. What would improve commas? More comma data, balanced sampling, or a dedicated comma model.
  15. How is punctuation evaluated? 1,500 test windows, token accuracy, boundary P/R/F1, per-class F1.

29.3 Ordering

  1. Why pairwise? Easy supervision, small model, and it generalizes to any document length.
  2. What is the input format? [CLS] sentence A [SEP] sentence B [SEP].
  3. What is the label? 1 if A precedes B, 0 otherwise.
  4. Why ORDER_MAX_LENGTH=192? Two 90-token sentences plus separators fit.
  5. What is Borda count? Each pair vote adds to the winner's score; sort by score.
  6. Why Borda over topological sort? Preferences can be cyclic; Borda tolerates cycles.
  7. What are the three decoders? Borda, clusters (greedy), beam (incremental).
  8. Which won? Borda: tau 0.2007 vs clusters 0.1223 and beam 0.0921.
  9. Why is tau low? Pairwise noise, cycles, and error propagation.
  10. What is Kendall's tau? Rank correlation between predicted and true order.
  11. Why a TF-IDF ablation? It isolates how much the neural model adds over lexical overlap.
  12. What did the ablation show? TF-IDF tau 0.0631, much lower than the model.
  13. How is ordering evaluated? Pair accuracy/macro F1 on 3,000 pairs, plus tau on 40 shuffled articles.
  14. Why 40 articles? It is enough for a stable tau estimate and cheap to run.
  15. What is the complexity? O(n²) pairs per article; for 100 sentences, ~4,950 pairs.

29.4 Translation

  1. Why IndicTrans2? Indic-specialized, strong Hindi→English, 200M is feasible.
  2. What is the language code pair? hin_Deva → eng_Latn.
  3. Why the toolkit? It handles Indic tokenization/spacing and the model's processor.
  4. Why not pass src_lang/tgt_lang to the tokenizer? This model does not take them; passing them breaks.
  5. What is _import_indic_processor? A compatibility shim for transformers 4 vs 5 import paths.
  6. Why return_attention_mask? IndicTrans2's forward needs it explicitly.
  7. Why clean_up_tokenization_spaces=False? It preserves Indic spacing.
  8. Why a local mirror? The official checkpoint is gated; a mirror was available.
  9. What are BLEU and chrF? n-gram precision/recall and character n-gram F-score.
  10. Why evaluate on IITB? It is a standard Hindi-English benchmark with references.
  11. What is the 256-token limit? The model's max positions; long sentences are truncated.

29.5 Engineering

  1. Why whitespace tokenization? \w+ drops Devanagari matras.
  2. Why pin transformers 4.44.2? transformers 5 removes the ONNX/tokenizer base path IndicTrans2 needs.
  3. Why pin peft 0.13.2? Newer peft imports torch.distributed.tensor, absent on macOS torch.
  4. Why pin datasets 3.6.0? datasets 4/5 require huggingface-hub ≥ 1.31, conflicting with the stack.
  5. Why eager attention on MPS? SDPA with dropout is broken on MPS.
  6. Why fp32 on MPS? fp16 is CUDA-only in this stack.
  7. How is memory capped? torch.cuda.set_per_process_memory_fraction(1.5/total).
  8. Why stage the models? Loading all three exceeds 1.5 GB.
  9. Why no fallback model? Silent fallbacks hide broken setups and hurt reproducibility.
  10. Why no LLM? Assignment constraint; also cost, latency, determinism.
  11. How is the pipeline aligned? Same sentence boundaries and GROUP_SIZE for Hindi and English.
  12. Why GROUP_SIZE=5? Groups are readable and match the expected output format.
  13. What happens to NULL? Removed, counted, reported.
  14. How do you reproduce? Pinned env, documented commands, saved reports, executed demo.
